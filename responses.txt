# Response to Classmates

Quinn, the middle child position is one that people seem to have strong opinions about, like it's some predetermined role you're forced to play. But I think it's interesting you mentioned it so directly. Middle children supposedly develop this ability to mediate, to navigate between different personalities. That makes sense to me. I grew up as an only child, so I learned to mediate too, but in a different way. When you're alone, you mediate between different versions of yourself, between who you want to be and who you actually are.

Painting is something I respect but have never been able to do. I spent years playing eSports semi-professionally before college, which meant I was staring at screens for six, seven, eight hours a day. My hand-eye coordination was excellent for things moving at 60 frames per second, but put a brush in my hand and suddenly those same hands that could execute frame-perfect combos turned into useless stumps. There's something about creating visual art that requires a different kind of patience. With eSports, you can practice a technique a thousand times in an afternoon. With painting, you have to let things develop, let the paint dry, accept that some things cannot be rushed.

Pharmacy school is serious. Pre-pharmacy track means organic chemistry, which I've heard described by biology majors as the class that makes people question their entire career path. Computer science has its own version of this. We call it data structures and algorithms. The class where you realize that writing code and actually understanding computation are two very different skills. But microbiology fascinates me in ways I didn't expect. Computers follow rules we invented. Microbes follow rules we're still discovering. Your field studies things that evolved over billions of years to survive in ways we can barely comprehend. My field studies things we created in the last seventy years and already can't fully control. Both are humbling in their own way.

Carter, hockey and broadcast journalism make complete sense together. You've spent your life in a world where split-second decisions matter, where you have to read patterns and anticipate what comes next. That translates directly to calling games. The best commentators aren't just describing what happened, they're explaining what's about to happen, why a player moved left instead of right, what the defense is setting up. That requires the same pattern recognition you use on ice.

Pat McAfee's model of broadcasting is interesting. He essentially rejected the traditional path, the one where you work your way up through local stations, pay your dues, wait for someone to die or retire before you get a real opportunity. Instead he built his own platform, controlled his own content, and made networks come to him. That's a philosophy that makes sense. You're already playing hockey at Penn State, which means you've built credibility. The broadcast desk will be there if you want it, but you're right to consider alternative paths.

Golf and Xbox with friends describes how most people actually relax, not how they think they should relax or how they post about relaxing on social media. Real leisure is repetitive. It's doing the same thing with the same people because the familiarity itself is the point. I play badminton casually most of the time for exactly this reason. This semester I'm trying to actually improve, which has made it less relaxing and more frustrating. Improvement requires you to break comfortable patterns, to fail at things you used to do automatically. That's the tradeoff. You can coast and enjoy yourself, or you can push yourself and feel worse in the short term while hoping it pays off later.

The July birthday thing is the kind of detail that sounds trivial but actually shapes how you experience family. You're the only one not born in July, which means your birthday is separate from everyone else's. That could feel isolating, like you're outside the pattern. Or it could feel special, like you get your own time without sharing it. Both are probably true depending on the year and the mood. These small accidents of timing, they matter more than people admit.

We're all doing different things but following similar patterns. We're pursuing specialized knowledge in our fields while maintaining activities that keep us connected to other people and to physical existence. Quinn paints and hangs out with friends. Carter golfs and plays Xbox. I play badminton and apparently destroyed my sleep schedule playing eSports for money before I was old enough to drink. The specifics differ but the structure is the same. We're trying to balance the person we're becoming professionally with the person we are socially and the person we are alone.

Your networking job lined up for summer, Carter's hockey career, Quinn's pharmacy school ambitions, these are the things we tell people about ourselves because they're legible. They make sense to parents and professors and future employers. But the other stuff matters too. The casual badminton, the painting, the Xbox sessions, the dogs named Cooper and Coco. Those are the things that make the professional development tolerable. The best-case scenario is that twenty years from now, we're successful in our respective fields and we still have time for the things that made college bearable. That's the goal anyway.
